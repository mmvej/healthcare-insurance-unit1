What the data tells us

These findings describe all 1,338 records. They stay fixed when you use the filters above; the charts and selected-group summary then show your chosen subset. Charges are displayed in USD using the dashboard's working assumption.

Smoking status has a large relationship with recorded charges. Mean charges are $32,050.23 for smoking=yes (274 records), compared with $8,434.27 for smoking=no (1,064 records). That is about 3.80 times as much, a difference of $23,615.96 per record. The median is also much higher: $34,456.35 versus $7,345.41. This is a broad difference between the distributions, rather than a gap in the means alone.

Older age is associated with higher charges, especially within non-smokers. The overall age–charge correlation is 0.30. When smoking groups are separated, it is 0.63 for smoking=no and 0.37 for smoking=yes. Age therefore has a clearer upward relationship with charges within non-smokers than the combined scatter plot first suggests. There is still substantial variation between records of the same age.

BMI and charges are strongly related among smokers, but only weakly related among non-smokers. The BMI–charge correlation is 0.81 in smoking=yes and 0.08 in smoking=no, compared with 0.20 overall. Higher-BMI smokers tend to have much higher charges in this sample. A single overall BMI figure hides this difference between the two groups; it does not show that the relationship is similar for everyone.

The southeast's higher average does not mean everyone there has higher charges. Southeast has the highest regional mean ($14,735.41), but northeast has the highest regional median ($10,057.65). Smoking=yes accounts for 25.0% of southeast records, compared with about 17.8% in northwest and southwest. Southeast also has the highest mean among smokers ($34,845.00), while northeast has the highest mean among non-smokers ($9,165.53). Regional rankings depend partly on which groups and which summary measure are compared.

The sex comparison changes when smoking status is considered. The male-labelled group has a higher overall mean ($13,956.75; 676 records) than the female-labelled group ($12,569.58; 662 records), but their medians are very close ($9,369.62 and $9,412.96). Smoking=yes is also more common in the male-labelled group (159 of 676 records) than the female-labelled group (115 of 662). Within non-smokers, the direction reverses: the female-labelled mean is $8,762.30 and the male-labelled mean is $8,087.20. The overall mean difference is therefore not evidence of a simple independent relationship between sex and charges.

More covered dependants do not show a clear, steadily increasing charge pattern. The correlation between dependant count and charges is only 0.07. Group means rise from $12,365.98 at zero dependants to $15,355.32 at three, then fall for four and five. Those last groups contain just 25 and 18 records, so their averages are less stable. The data does not support a rule that charges consistently increase with every extra dependant.

Correlation here means Pearson's linear correlation: values near 0 indicate little linear relationship, while values closer to 1 indicate a stronger positive relationship. These are descriptive comparisons, not fully adjusted estimates. Diagnoses, treatment intensity and other missing factors may explain part of the patterns.

What this means for planning: use smoking-group distributions and age/BMI comparisons to structure further investigation, and compare regions within groups before relying on a regional average. Use both mean and median because high charges pull the overall mean ($13,270.42) above the median ($9,382.03). None of these associations establishes what caused an individual's charges or justifies an individual pricing decision.

What each variable means

A variable is a field recorded for each row. The original dataset contains seven variables; age band is an extra field created for this analysis. Each row is a source record, not a verified unique person.

These are analysis groups, not clinical categories. The final band is narrower because this sample ends at 64.

Records (n)
The number of rows in the selected group. Matching rows are retained because there is no ID proving that they describe the same person.

Mean
The sum of the selected charge amounts divided by the number of records. Large values can pull the mean upwards.

Median
The middle charge after sorting the values. With an even number of records, it is the average of the two middle values.

Standard deviation (SD)
A measure of how spread out the charges are around the mean, in the same charge units. The table uses sample SD; it is undefined for a single record.

Quartiles and IQR
The first and third quartiles mark the 25th and 75th percentiles. Their difference, the interquartile range (IQR), describes the spread of the middle half of the values.

Association
A pattern in how variables vary together. A difference between groups does not, by itself, prove that one variable caused the difference in charges.

Definitions follow the dataset publisher's description and the inspected CSV. Ranges describe the full downloaded file, not the current filter selection.

Source, limitations and project files

This is a secondary, public teaching dataset: Healthcare Insurance by willian oliveira, version 1, downloaded 27 October 2026. The metadata lists CC0. The original file has seven fields and no missing values. One repeated row is retained because there is no person identifier to prove an error.

The source does not confirm its observation dates, sampling frame or exact geographic boundaries. There are no diagnoses, treatments or enrolment periods. Observed differences are associations. They cannot establish causes, future expenses or appropriate prices or eligibility decisions for individuals.
